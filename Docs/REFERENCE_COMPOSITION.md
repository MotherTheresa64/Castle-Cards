# Castle Cards — Gameplay Camera / Reference Composition

This is the visual composition lock for the first playable medieval-tavern arena.

## Core identity

Castle Cards is first and foremost a **physical tabletop card battler that becomes a miniature fantasy war on the table**. The closest design shorthand is the feeling of a card game where played cards manifest into creatures, spells, defenses and terrain — but the visual identity must remain original to Castle Cards.

This is **not** an RTS viewed from above and it is **not** a pre-filled battlefield simulator. The player should feel seated across from another person, holding cards, committing pieces to terrain, watching those choices become physical miniature combat, and protecting a castle/king at their end of the table.

The battlefield therefore needs empty strategic space. A match should visibly build up as cards are played, troops are deployed, spells alter terrain and upgrades reshape the castle.

## Non-negotiable frame

- The player is seated at a physical war table in a dark, warm medieval tavern.
- The battlefield is a long miniature diorama that runs away from the player toward the opponent.
- The board should fill most of the frame. Tavern architecture is framing and atmosphere, not the subject.
- The player castle anchors the lower foreground and must **not** dominate the middle of the screen.
- The enemy castle sits at the far end of the board, smaller through perspective.
- A fully modeled brunette, bearded opponent is visibly seated behind the enemy castle and is one of the primary focal points.
- The opponent must read as a real player across the table, with hands/forearms near the tabletop, not as a giant statue behind the map.
- Cards are physical tabletop objects. The player's hand lives near the lower edge of the frame and should never become a giant UI tray.
- Played creature cards should resolve into collectible tabletop miniatures on the landscape.
- Played spell cards should visibly affect the physical board: scorch marks, healing fields, ice, broken terrain, burning forests, etc.
- Terrain/defense cards should physically alter the landscape and castle rather than merely changing numbers in a HUD.
- Troops are collectible tabletop miniatures distributed in sparse, readable commitments and skirmish groups, not chess-piece rows or a fully populated RTS army.
- Terrain is sculpted and patchy with roads, water, rocks, trees, cover, chokepoints and readable tactical routes.
- The battlefield should have room for the match to grow. Empty land is intentional gameplay space.
- Tavern architecture frames the scene with shelves, timber, chains, lanterns, barrels and weapons, but never hides the opponent or battlefield.
- Warm lantern/fire light is the key; cool ambient fill preserves readable detail in the shadows.
- HUD is secondary to the physical game and must remain small, translucent and mostly peripheral.

## Quality hierarchy

When a visual change conflicts with this composition, protect these in order:

1. Opponent face / seated-player silhouette readability.
2. The physical-card-game feeling: cards at the player edge becoming pieces on the table.
3. Full-board readability from player castle to enemy castle.
4. Player castle as a low foreground anchor rather than a screen-blocking centerpiece.
5. Unit silhouettes and active skirmish readability.
6. Terrain routes / hazards / upgrade state.
7. Tabletop props and cheat-system props.
8. Tavern clutter.
9. HUD decoration.

## Progression rule

Upgrades should physically alter the miniature board. Walls, towers, moat/lava, traps, reinforcement structures, troop equipment, terrain density and spell aftermath should appear on the diorama itself rather than only as UI statistics.

A run should visibly evolve from a relatively sparse starting battlefield into a personalized tabletop war machine built from the player's choices.

## Future areas

This composition is the baseline for future areas. Forest, mines, prison, desert, polar, Asian-inspired, evil castle, seaport, floating-island, steampunk, royal castle and dragon-lair arenas can change architecture, opponents, troops, terrain and lighting while preserving the same readable seated-tabletop relationship and card-to-miniature gameplay language.
